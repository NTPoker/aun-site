The JLPT and speaking

Passed JLPT N2 but can’t speak Japanese?
Six things to check

The JLPT does not measure speaking. So passing N2 or N1 does not tell you how well you speak. If you feel you “can’t speak”, splitting that into six parts shows where you actually get stuck.

The reason is in what the test looks at

The JLPT tests language knowledge (vocabulary and grammar), reading and listening. You answer by choosing from the options given. There is no speaking test (see Does the JLPT have a speaking test?).

Reading and listening are about taking in someone else’s words. To speak, you have to recall the words yourself, build the sentence, and say it in sounds the other person can understand. These are different skills.

JLPT score reports show a CEFR level for reference, but the official site says it does not include speaking, writing or interaction (JLPT official site, Indication of the CEFR Level for Reference, checked 13 November 2026).

Passing N2 shows language knowledge, reading and listening. It says nothing about your speaking, good or bad. If you passed and still feel you can’t speak, it does not necessarily mean you studied the wrong way. It means there is a skill you have not checked yet.

Split “can’t speak” into six

“Can’t speak” is not one thing. AUN looks at speaking as six skills. See which one sounds like you.

SkillYou are stuck here if…
ContentYou answer the question, but in a few words. Reasons and specifics do not come out
OrganisationThe order gets mixed up as you talk, and the listener loses the thread
FluencyYou know what you want to say, but the pauses are long and you restart often
GrammarYou understand the form when you read it, but cannot finish the sentence when you speak. Particles and verb forms slow you down
VocabularyWords you know do not come when you need them, and you stop instead of rephrasing
PronunciationYou said the sentence, but people ask you to repeat it, or hear a different word

What to fix next depends on which one is holding you back. Someone whose words do not come needs something different from someone who is asked to repeat because of pronunciation.

How to check by yourself

All you need is the voice recorder on your phone.

  1. Pick one question. For example: “Tell me about something that made you happy recently”, or “Explain your job (or what you study)”.
  2. Speak for about a minute without preparing, and record it. If you read out a written answer, it is hard to check whether you can choose words and build sentences on the spot.
  3. Wait before you listen. The next day, if you can.
  4. Check the six points below.
ContentDid you answer the question with reasons and specifics?
OrganisationCan you follow the order without getting lost?
FluencyDo long pauses or restarts break up what you say?
GrammarDo you finish your sentences?
VocabularyDid you stop because a word did not come?
PronunciationIs any part hard to make out?

The limit of listening to yourself

When you listen to your own recording, you already know what you meant. That makes it hard to notice what did not get across. The same goes for pronunciation: a sound that is clear to you may not be clear to someone else. Whether it got across is something to check with another person.

Have someone else listen

There are three ways.

  • Take a speaking test. Suited to when a school or an employer needs a result.
  • Ask a teacher. If you take lessons, a teacher can tell you your weak points and how to work on them.
  • Have a Japanese person listen to a recording. Suited to when you want an easy way to find out how you sound.

The differences are set out in Three ways to check your spoken Japanese.

Frequently asked questions

If I passed N1, can I speak Japanese?

Passing does not tell you. N1 shows language knowledge, reading and listening. The JLPT does not measure speaking.

Why can I understand Japanese but not speak it?

To listen, it is enough to understand the other person’s words. To speak, you have to recall the words yourself, build the sentence, and say it in sounds the other person can understand. These are different skills.

Which of the six should I fix first?

When checking by yourself, note down what caught your ear as you listen; that shows which skill tends to hold you back. An AUN result names one “fix next” skill. It is chosen from the skills that fall short in the situations marked △ or ×, with situations close to ○ counting more (see How AUN results are decided).

Will AUN tell me my JLPT level?

No. AUN looks at speaking, and its scores do not correspond to JLPT levels N5 to N1.
